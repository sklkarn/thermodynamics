    Section 2.1 First Law

    The first law of thermodynamics states that the change in internal energy of a system is equal to the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system on its surroundings. When a system undergoes a process, the change in internal energy \(\Delta U\) is given by \(dU = dQ - dW\text{,}\) where \(dQ\) is the heat added to the system and \(dW\) is the work done by the system. This law is a statement of the conservation of energy, which means that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or converted from one form to another. In another way, in every cyclic process, the net heat absorbed by the system is equal to the net work done by the system. Therefore, the internal energy of the system remains unchanged after a cyclic process, i.e.,
    \begin{equation} \oint \,dU = 0\text{.}\tag{2.1.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    For any system if value of \(U\) at initial state, O is \(U_o\text{,}\) the \(U\) is a fuction of the state, X as
    \begin{equation*} U=U(T, V,N_k) +U_o \end{equation*}
    For an open system
    \begin{equation} \,dU = \,dQ+ \,dW + \,dU_{matter}\tag{2.1.2} \end{equation}
    here \(\,dU_{matter}\) is additional flow of matter. The total change in internal energy of a system can be expressed as the sum of the changes in internal energy due to heat transfer, work done, and matter flow.
    🔗
    If the system is open and going from state A to state B, the change in internal energy can be expressed as
    \begin{equation*} \int \,dU = U_B - U_A \end{equation*}
    Since \(U\) is a state function, the change in internal energy depends only on the initial and final states of the system, and not on the path taken to get from one state to the other. In general, the change in total internal energy can be written as the sum of the changes in internal energy due to heat transfer, work done, and matter flow,
    \begin{equation*} \,dU = \,dQ - P \,dV + \phi \, dq +E\,dP -B\,dM+ +\gamma \,dA + \cdots \end{equation*}
    where \(\phi \,dq\) is the energy due to transfer of charge \(\,dq\text{,}\) and \(E\,dP\) is the energy due to transfer of polarization \(dP\text{,}\) \(-B\,dM\) is the change in energy due to change in magnetic dipole moment, \(\,dM\text{,}\) and \(\gamma \,dA\) is the energy due to change in surface area \(\,dA\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For a system undergoing chemical transformations, the total energy \(U=U(T,V, N_k)\) can be expressed as
    \begin{equation} \,dU = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_{V, N_k} \,dT + \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_{T, N_k} \,dV + \sum_k \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial N_k} \right)_{T, V, N_{j\neq k}} \,dN_k \tag{2.1.3} \end{equation}
    where \(dN_k\) is the change in the number of moles of the \(k\)-th component.
    🔗
    Since no work is done at constant volume, \(\,dU=\,dQ\text{.}\) Hence,
    \begin{equation*} C_{mV} (T,V) = \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial T} \right)_{V} = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_{V,N=1} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Mechanical Work due to Change in Volume: When a system undergoes a change in volume \(\,dV\) under the action of a pressure \(P\text{,}\) the work done by the system is given by \(dW = - P \cdot dV\text{.}\) This is known as the mechanical work done by the system. The negative sign indicates, the volume increase as work is done by the system on its surroundings, and positive sign indicates the decrease of volume of the system when the work is done on the system by its surroundings.
    🔗
    Isothermal Volume Change: When a system undergoes an isothermal process, the temperature remains constant.
    \begin{equation} Work = \int_{V_i}^{V_f} - P \,dV = -NRT \ln \frac{V_f}{V_i}\tag{2.1.4} \end{equation}
    The negative sign indicates that an expanding gas transfers energy to the surroundings, while a compressing gas absorbs energy from the surroundings.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.1 Relation between \(C_{mp}\) and \(C_{mv}\)

    The change in internal energy for one-component substance (2.1.7):
    \begin{equation} \,dU = \,dQ-p\,dV = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_{V} \,dT + \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_{T} \,dV \tag{2.1.5} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation} or, \quad \,dQ = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_{V} \,dT +\left[p+ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_{T} \right] \,dV\tag{2.1.6} \end{equation}
    🔗
    If the gas is heated at constant volume, then no work is done and the change in energy is due to heat supplied to the gas.
    \begin{equation} C_{V} = \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial T} \right)_{V} = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_{V}\tag{2.1.7} \end{equation}
    However, if the gas is heated at constant pressure, then
    \begin{equation} C_{P} = \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial T} \right)_{p} = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_{V} + \left[p+ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_{T} \right] \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p\tag{2.1.8} \end{equation}
    Hence,
    \begin{equation} C_p-C_V = \left[p+ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_{T} \right] \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p\tag{2.1.9} \end{equation}
    The right hand term is due to additional heat needed to raise the temperature of a gas at constant pressure to compentsate energy due to volume expansion.
    🔗
    For and ideal gas where \(U\) is only a function of temperature and is independent of the volume, hence, \(\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_{T} = 0\) and for 1 mole of a gas, \(pV=RT\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} p\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p = R. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, molar heat capacities reduces to
    \begin{equation} C_{mp} - C_{mv} = R\tag{2.1.10} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.2 Adiabatic Processes in Ideal Gas

    In adiabatic process, the state of a system changes wihout any exchange of heat. Hence from (2.1.5),
    \begin{equation*} \,dQ = \, dU +p\,dV \end{equation*}
    or,
    \begin{equation} 0 = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_{V} \,dT +\left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_{T} \,dV +p\, dV \tag{2.1.11} \end{equation}
    For an ideal gas, since \(U\) is a function of temperature only at constant volume, we have
    \begin{equation*} nC_{mv} \,dT + p\,dV = 0 \end{equation*}
    If volume changes in ideal gas equation during the process of change, then we have
    \begin{equation*} PV = nRT \Rightarrow \, nC_{mv} \,dT + \frac{nRT}{V}\,dV =0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{align*} C_{mv} \,dT + \frac{RT}{V}\,dV \amp =0 \\ or, \, \frac{\,dT}{T} + \frac{R}{C_{mv} V}\,dV \amp =0 \\ or, \, \frac{\,dT}{T} + \frac{C_{mp}-C_{mv}}{C_{mv} V}\,dV \amp =0 \\ or, \, \frac{\,dT}{T} + \left(\frac{C_{mp}}{C_{mv}}-1\right)\frac{\,dV}{V} \amp =0\\ or, \, \frac{\,dT}{T} + \left(\gamma - 1\right)\frac{\,dV}{V} \amp =0 \, \quad \left[\text { define} \frac{C_{mp}}{C_{mv}} =\gamma \right] \\ or, \, \frac{\,dT}{T} \amp = - \left(\gamma - 1\right)\frac{\,dV}{V}\\ or, \, \ln T \amp =-\left(\gamma -1\right)\ln V + \text{ constant} \end{align*}
    \begin{equation} \therefore \, TV^{(\gamma-1)} = \text{ constant} \tag{2.1.12} \end{equation}
    Also,
    \begin{equation} or, \, pV^{\gamma} = \text{ constant} \tag{2.1.13} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation} or, \, T^\gamma p^{1-\gamma} = \text{ constant} \tag{2.1.14} \end{equation}
    can be derived from \(pV=nRT\)
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.1.2.1 Sound Propagation

    The propagation of sound in nature is also an adiabatic process because sound is pressure wave and propagate in the medium without generating heat. The loudness or internsity level of sound is given by https://sklkarn.github.io/calphysii/sec_fourth-sound.html
    \begin{equation*} \beta = 10 \log_{10} \left(\frac{I}{I_o}\right) = 10 \log_{10} \left(\frac{p^2_{rms}}{p^2_o}\right) \end{equation*}
    where \(p_o= 2\times 10^{-8} \) kPa, \(p_{rms} = \sqrt{(p-p_{atm})^2}\)
    🔗
    The velocity of sound is given by
    \begin{equation*} v_s = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}} \end{equation*}
    where \(\rho \) is density of the medium and \(B=\frac{stress}{\text{ vol. strain}} = -\frac{dp}{dV/V}\text{.}\) Negative sign indicates volume is decreasing with the increase of pressure.
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \, B= -V\frac{dp}{dV} = \gamma p \end{equation*}
    \begin{align*} pV^\gamma \amp = constant \\ p\gamma V^{\gamma-1}\, dV +V^\gamma \, dp \amp = 0 \\ \frac{dp}{dV} \amp = -\frac{p\gamma V^{\gamma -1}}{v^\gamma} = -\frac{\gamma p}{V} \end{align*}
    Now from,
    \begin{equation*} p=\frac{nRT}{V} = \frac{mRT}{MV} = \frac{\rho RT}{M} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \, B =\frac{ \gamma \rho RT}{M} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation} v_s = \sqrt{\frac{ \gamma RT}{M}}\tag{2.1.15} \end{equation}

    Subsubsection 2.1.2.2 Hess’s law

    Hess’s Law of Constant Heat Summation states that the total enthalpy change (\(\Delta H\)) for a chemical reaction is the same whether the reaction occurs in one step or in a series of steps. This is a direct consequence of the conservation of energy (First Law of Thermodynamics) and the fact that enthalpy is a state function. Because enthalpy depends only on the initial and final states of a system and not the path taken between them. We can manipulate a series of chemical equations like algebraic equations to find the net enthalpy change of a target reaction.
    🔗
    Hess’s law refers to the heat evolved in a chemical reaction under constant pressure. Under such conditions, a part of the energy released during the reaction may be converted to work \(W = -\int_{V_1}^{V_2} p \, dV\) , if there is a change in volume from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\text{.}\) Using the basic equation of the First Law, \(dU = dQ - V\, dp\text{,}\) the heat evolved \(\Delta Q_p\) during a chemical transformation at a constant pressure can be written as
    \begin{equation*} \Delta Q_p = \int_{U_1}^{U_2} \, dU + \int_{V_1}^{V_2} p \, dV = \left(U_2-U_1\right)+p\left(V_2-V_1\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \, \Delta Q_p = \left(U_2-pV_1\right) - \left(U_1+ pV_1\right) \end{equation*}
    Since \(U,p,V\) are specified by the state of the system not by the path, we can say \(U+pV\) is a state function. Hence, the state function, \(U+pV\) is called the Enthalpy, \(H\). The heat released by a chemical reaction at constant pressure
    \begin{equation*} \Delta Q_p = H_f - H_i \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} H \equiv U+pV \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since
    \begin{equation*} dQ_p =\,dp+p\,dV =dH_p \end{equation*}
    If the system consists of one mole of a substance, and if the change in temperature due to the exchange of heat is \(dT\text{,}\) then it follows that
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{dQ}{dT}\right)_p = C_{mp} = \left(\frac{dH_m}{dT}\right)_p \end{equation*}
